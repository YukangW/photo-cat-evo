1. Introduction 

It is generally acknowledged that the use of environmentally 
compatible and renewable energy conversion schemes is the optimal 
choice for long-term sustainability [1–3]. Due to its innumerable advantages, such as convenient storage, transportation, and high energy 
density, hydrogen energy has the highest acceptance among the many 
energy resources to alleviate the energy crisis [4,5]. Nowadays, most 
hydrogen production technologies are still dependent on fossil energy 
consumption. Thus, the challenge of how to accelerate the transition to 
hydrogen production is of extraordinary significance. Hydrogen (H2) 
generation from photocatalytic water splitting using solar light is one of 
the most desirable means since solar energy is inexhaustible and 
non-polluting [6–10]. Hydrogen prepared by photocatalysis technology 
has high purity and can be directly employed. In addition, great efforts 
have been devoted to addressing the environmental crisis, photocatalytic technologies are also favored for the cleanup of environmental 
contaminants, such as the degradation of organic dyes, organic fluorides, and antibiotic that are difficult to detoxify by microorganisms, and 

the reduction of heavy metals [11–17]. Nevertheless, the core of photocatalytic technology is the photocatalyst, so it is indispensable to 
design a highly efficient and stable photocatalyst to reduce the ecological damage. 

To achieve this goal, considerable photocatalysts have been explored 
by researchers, and their feasibility in practical applications has been 
investigated and verified [18–24]. Among all the photocatalysts, g-C3N4 
has attracted the most interest from researchers thanks to its exceptional 
graphite-like two-dimensional layered structure [25,26]. According to 
the recent reports, the C and N atoms in g-C3N4 are bonded by covalent 
bonds, similar to the hexagonal honeycomb structure of the benzene 
ring. The ring is connected with N atoms, and the layers are stacked via 
hydrogen bonds to form a blocky structure, which endows g-C3N4 stable 
physical and chemical properties [27]. Simultaneously, the multiple 
advantages of g-C3N4, such as low cost, simple preparation process, 
non-toxicity, and suitable bandgap, make g-C3N4 a promising material 
in the development of photocatalytic technology utilizing solar energy 
[28–31]. However, as a single photocatalyst, g-C3N4 demonstrates low 
photocatalytic activity owing to a low specific surface area, and sluggish

separation and transfer of photoinduced carriers [32–35]. Therefore, to 
meet the practical application, multiple methods have been explored 
and tried to further ameliorate the photocatalytic properties of g-C3N4, 
among which the main approaches include atomic doping [36], deposition of precious metals [37], morphology regulation [38] and construction of defects [39]. Therein, the introduction of defects 
demonstrates to be a reliable approach to enhance the activity of pure 
g-C3N4 in many aspects [40,41]. Amongst those strategies, the studies on 
g-C3N4 with defects have been a focal point in the photocatalytic field in 
recent years. Defects can be introduced in the bulk or on the surface of 
photocatalysts. Therein, surface vacancies, as one of the defects can 
capture electrons, and promote the transfer of photogenerated electrons 
on the interface [42]. Simultaneously, these sites after the escape of 
surface atoms can also be used as reaction sites, which can greatly 
enhance the absorption of the target species and broaden the light 
response spectrum [43]. For example, Zhang’s research team prepared 
ultra-thin carbon nitride with abundant carbon defects on the surface, 
the materials can realize strong adsorption and activate dinitrogen 
molecules. The carbon defects accelerate the photogenerated charge 
separation between bulk and surface, thereby displaying increased 
photocatalytic nitrogen fixation activity under visible-light irradiation 
[44]. Furthermore, Wu and colleagues successfully designed nitrogen 
-deficient carbon nitride by means of hydrothermal copolymerization, 
which endows g-C3N4 with a wider range of visible-light response and a 
larger exposed surface area for the photocatalytic reduction reaction, 
enabling efficient separation of photogenerated carriers. H2 evolution 
rate over the photocatalyst prepared can reach 92 μmol h−1 with the 
optimal conditions [45]. Additionally, vacancies can also affect the 
adsorption properties of target pollutants, thereby affecting the coordination structure and electronic state of target pollutants [46]. More 
importantly, the location, structure, and concentration of vacancies are 
vital factors that affect photocatalytic performance. For example, g-C3N4 
photocatalysts with different levels of nitrogen vacancies exhibit 
distinguished improvement in hydrogen evolution and pollutant 
degradation as reported by Liang et al. [47]. 

Many methods have been developed to introduce vacancies into g- 
C3N4, such as baking in a reductive atmosphere [48], morphology 
control [49], and construction of heterojunctions [50]. However, all 
these approaches have inherent shortcomings, such as high economic 
cost and complicated processes. Thus, the development of a simple 
approach to constructing vacancies is an important challenge. 

Inspired by the above reports, g-C3N4 was surface treated through 
NaBH4 solution with different concentrations to introduce carbon vacancies to boost the visible-light-driven photocatalytic reduction performance. The crystal property, microscopic appearance, and surface 
states of the treated photocatalysts were studied and analyzed by multiple characterization methods. The impact of carbon vacancies on the 
optical properties of g-C3N4 was explored through spectroscopic measurement and theoretical calculations. The photocatalytic efficiency of 
the sample was evaluated with visible light illumination inducing water 
splitting for the H2 production and Cr(VI) reduction experiments. Using 
3NBCN as the representative of the photocatalyst prepared, the stability 
of g-C3N4 after NaBH4 treatment was explored through four photocatalytic hydrogen production cycle experiments. The results show that 
carbon vacancies can significantly facilitate photoinduced e−/h+ pairs 
to separate and migrate, expanding the overall light absorption and 
utilization, and thus enhancing the photocatalytic performance. Finally, 
the mechanism for the enhanced photocatalytic property was explored 
based on all the observed results. In all, this work develops a practical 
strategy to boost the photocatalytic performance of g-C3N4. 

2. Experiment section 

2.1. Preparation and treatment of g-C3N4 

g-C3N4 was prepared successfully according to the synthesis route 

presented in Scheme 1. Specifically, 10 g melamine was completely 
ground up and transferred to a crucible with cover and placed into a 
temperature-programmed the muffle furnace for heat treatment for 210 
min to 550 ◦C for 4 h [51]. The calcined sample was ground again to 
obtain a light-yellow g-C3N4 precursor material after the material cooled 
down to 25 ◦C. Subsequently, the desired NaBH4 solution (10.0 mol L−1) 
was diluted with NaOH solution (pH≈10.0) to a specific concentration. 
2 g g-C3N4 precursor was added into 100 mL NaBH4 solution with a 
different concentration (2.0 mol L−1, 3.0 mol L−1, 4.0 mol L−1 and 5.0 
mol L−1), and the suspension was intensely agitated at 25 ◦C for a day. 
The treated samples were fully cleaned with large amounts of 
high-purity water and anhydrous ethanol to eliminate residual surface 
impurities. Finally, the catalyst was dried and ground and was named 
2NBCN, 3NBCN, 4NBCN, and 5NBCN in light of the concentration of 
NaBH4 solution used, respectively. The reference g-C3N4 was also 
treated as the above treatment method without the presence of NaBH4 
and denoted as GCN. 

2.2. Characterization of the photocatalysts 

An SSA-4200 automatic analyzer was used to test the N2 adsorption- 
desorption curves, specific surface area, and pore structure using BET 
analysis. The test temperature was 77 K. The relative content of each 
element in the sample was analyzed by energy dispersive spectroscopy 
(EDS). The morphology of the prepared photocatalysts was directly 
observed through a VEGA3 SBU scanning electron microscope (SEM) 
and transmission electron microscope (TEM), the acceleration voltage of 
the instrument was 5 kV and the current was 5 A. The crystal structure 
and phase composition of solid powder were recorded via an X-ray 
diffraction instrument (XRD). To investigate the changes in the geometric structure of the sample, Raman spectra of the prepared photocatalysts were scanned on a DXR Raman instrument. An XSAM 800 X-ray 
photoelectron spectrometer (XPS) was employed to characterize the 
surface properties of the catalyst and the micro-chemical environment of 
elements. The test voltage was 12 kV, and the test current was 12 mA. 
The relative content of unpaired electrons of the prepared photocatalysts was performed on a low-temperature electron spin resonance 
(LT-ESR) spectrometer (model: Bruker ESR A 300). The optical properties of all samples were tested via UH4150 UV–visible diffuse reflection 
(UV–Vis DRS) with pure BaSO4 as the reference sample, and the scanning range was 200 nm–800 nm. Surface photovoltage spectroscopy 
(SPS) can directly and graphically demonstrate the photoinduced e−/h+

pairs separation and migration behavior in semiconductor materials on 
a homemade instrument. Finally, the photoelectric properties of all the 
prepared catalysts in a liquid phase environment (Na2SO4 solution, pH 
=
7) were investigated on an electrochemical station CHI660 
instrument.

3. Results and discussion 

Specific surface area (SBET) and pore volume of the photocatalyst 
directly determine the adsorption capacity of the target species and the 
utilization rate of light, the surface characteristics of reference GCN and 
3NBCN were analyzed by BET test. The SBET of GCN and 3NBCN were 
straightway calculated by the BET analyzer to be 22.5 m2/g and 22.8 
m2/g, separately, which suggests that surface treatment of g-C3N4 by 
sodium borohydride solution cannot effectively change the SBET of g- 
C3N4. The N2 adsorption-desorption curves and pore radius distribution 
of GCN and 3NBCN samples were further analyzed. As illustrated in 

Fig. 1A and B, the adsorption-desorption curves have obvious hysteresis 
loops, which pertain to the type IV isotherm, implying that both samples 
have mesoporous structures [52,53]. The particle size distribution (PSD) 
curves of the two samples are mainly concentrated in the region of 2 
nm–50 nm, demonstrating that mesopores exist in the photocatalysts. 
However, the 3NBCN sample shows a slightly different pore adsorption 
capacity compared with the GCN sample. The different pores can 
effectively change photocatalytic performance by introducing surface 
defects [54], which can be attested by EDS, XPS, and LT-ESR test results. 

Micromorphology characteristics of GCN and 3NBCN samples were 
observed by SEM. As present in Fig. 2A and B, the micromorphology of 
GCN and 3NBCN (B) sample demonstrates a block-like structure formed 
by a rough surface and irregular layered stacking. Furthermore, it was 
observed that the uneven surfaces of both have fault lines, which provide additional sites for the catalyst to contact the reactant. Simultaneously, the micro-morphology of the sample was deeply observed by 
TEM and shown in Fig. 2C and D, two samples were in disordered state. 
Compared with GCN, 3NBCN has thinner nanolayer and tighter stack, 
indicating that the microstructure of g-C3N4 has been altered after 
NaBH4 treatment. 

XRD analysis is a prerequisite to evaluate the crystal structure and 
differences of the photocatalysts prepared [55]. To investigate the 
crystal structural changes of g-C3N4 before and after NaBH4 treatment, 
the prepared samples were analyzed by XRD. As revealed in Fig. 3A, 
XRD patterns show two representative diffraction peaks at 2θ = 12.9

◦

and 27.4

◦, belonging to the (100) and (002) crystal plane of g-C3N4, 
which can be assigned to the orderly arrangement of thiazine basic units 
in g-C3N4 structures and the interlayer closely connecting the triazine 
ring structures [56]. Compared with the pure g-C3N4, the diffraction 
peak corresponding to the (002) crystal planes of all the NaBH4 treated 
g-C3N4 shifts to a higher angle, and 3NBCN displays the obvious shift, 
implying that the interlayer spacing becomes smaller, which is beneficial to shorten the migration time of photogenerated electrons from the 
bulk to the surface, thereby more electrons can participate in the redox 
reaction, effectively boosting the photocatalytic activity.

treatment. Moreover, the relative proportion of elements in GCN and

In order to further explore the authentic existence of enriched C 
vacancies after g-C3N4 treatment by NaBH4, XPS was used to survey the 
elemental environment of the sample and exhibited in Fig. 4A. Three 
elements were present in both samples. C and N are attributed to the 
composition of the g-C3N4 ontology, whereas the presence of element O 
originates from the adsorption of O2 and H2O on the sample surface. 
Fig. 4B is the high-resolution C 1s spectrum of reference GCN and 
3NBCN, which can be fitted into two peaks, and the binding energy is 
287.9 eV and 284.7 eV, 287.8 eV, and 284.6 eV, respectively, allocating 
to C-(N)2 and C–C species in the molecular structure [60]. By comparison, it can be found that the C-(N)2 area of 3NBCN (49.15%) is much 
smaller than that of GCN (62.45%), which can substantially explain the 
decrease of carbon content in 3NBCN. These results powerfully illustrate 
the existence of C vacancies [61]. From Fig. 4C, the three core peak 
positions of the N 1s spin orbits of the two samples were fitted to 404.2 
eV, 400.2 eV, and 398.4 eV, respectively, which can be assigned to the 
–NH2, N-(C)3, and C=N–C in the molecular composition of the sample, 
respectively. Interestingly, the peak area of –NH2 in 3NBCN is slightly 

higher than that in GCN, which can be assigned to the formation of C 
vacancies, leading to the desaturation of the originally connected N 
atom, thus binding available H atoms as compensation, and forming 
more –NH2 [62]. In the two samples, the corresponding peak areas for 
N-(C)3 and N-(C)2 also have significant changes. This prominent phenomenon provides strong support for the DFT calculations performed in 
this work. Compared with GCN, the N 1s orbital of 3NBCN moves towards a lower binding energy because of the abundant carbon vacancies 
induced by the intermolecular charge interaction. In order to more 
accurately confirm the existence of carbon vacancies, the LT-ESR test 
was performed. As exhibited in Fig. 4D, GCN and 3NBCN sample display 
two conspicuous Lorentzian lines near g = 2.003, which highlights the 
generation of vacancies. Combining EDS and XPS results, one can 
conclude that the vacancies are C vacancies. Furthermore, 3NBCN has a 
higher ESR signal than GCN, which illustrates that 3NBCN holds more 
unpaired electrons, further confirming that abundant C vacancies have 
been successfully introduced. Commonly, the presence of carbon vacancies is beneficial to improve the electron delocalization ability, 
promote the separation efficiency of photoinduced e−/h+ pairs and 
accelerate the directional transfer of electrons, resulting in effectively 
enhanced photocatalytic activity [63].

In order to verify the effect of the presence of abundant C vacancies 
on the band structure of the photocatalyst, DFT calculations were performed. Since the internal atoms of g-C3N4 are arranged in order and 
have high symmetry, C and N atoms can be divided into two types of C 
and three types of N, which can be labeled as C l, C 2, N l, N 2, and N 3, 
respectively, as depicted in Fig. 6A. According to the XPS spectrum results of N 1s, the content of N-(C)2 in 3NBCN decreases dramatically, 
suggesting that the C vacancies can be caused by the absence of C 1 
atoms. Namely, no C 1 atoms exist in the structure of 3NBCN when 
optimizing the computational model. Fig. 6B displays the calculated 
band structure of GCN after geometric optimization, and the Eg of the 
reference g-C3N4 is 1.60 eV, which is lower than the experimental value 
due to PBE functional usually underestimates the actual value of the 
band gap from its limitations [68–70]. The calculated Eg of 3NBCN is 
only 1.28 eV (Fig. 6D), The obvious decreased trend of the bandgap is 
highly in agreement with the experimental results, which solidly validates that the existence of C defects successfully lowers the bandgap. 
Fig. 6C and E illustrate the density of states (DOS) of GCN and 3NBCN, 
respectively. It is clear that the N-p orbital mainly contributes to the VB 
of both samples, while the CB is determined by both C-p and N-p orbitals 
[71,72].

The photocatalytic activities of all the photocatalysts were evaluated 
via measuring visible-light-driven water splitting for H2 evolution and 
the outcomes are depicted in Fig. 8A. Under visible light exposure for 4 
h, the cumulative H2 evolution amount on all the g-C3N4 samples treated 
by NaBH4 is higher than that of GCN. The variation law of H2 evolution 
efficiency over the prepared samples is further analyzed (Fig. 8B). It can 
be seen that 3NBCN with outstanding photoelectric properties shows the 
highest H2 evolution rate, reaching 1764.9 μmol g−1 h−1, which is nearly 
8.56 folds of that of reference GCN (206.3 μmol g−1 h−1). Interestingly, 
with the further increase of NaBH4 concentration, the H2 evolution efficiency begins to drop significantly. The H2 evolution efficiency of 
4NBCN and 5NBCN is only 1158.8 μmol g−1 h−1 and 1046.5 μmol g−1 

h−1, respectively. The reason for the considerable decrease in photocatalytic activity can be attributed to the excessive C vacancies, which 
will become the electron recombination center, hindering the smooth 
transmission of electrons, leading to the reduction of the transfer path of 
photogenerated electrons, and thus the number of electrons that can 
reach CB is seriously reduced. The enhanced photocatalytic H2 evolution 
performance of g-C3N4 can be attributed to the formation of an appropriate amount of C vacancies, which increases the adsorption sites, expands the range of light absorption, reduces the electron transition gap, 

and further accelerates the photoinduced charge pairs separate and 
migrate. To investigate the stability of g-C3N4 treated, the 3NBCN 
sample was investigated for four cycles of photocatalytic H2 generated. 
The H2 evolution amount on the 3NBCN sample remains at a high level 
after four cycles (Fig. 8C). Meanwhile, Fig. 8D shows the XRD pattern of 
the sample after four cycles. Compared with the original sample, no new 
peaks were detected, which means that the g-C3N4 photocatalysts have 
exceptional stability. Moreover, the photocatalytic hydrogen production 
activity of 3NBCN was compared with reported g-C3N4-based photocatalysts, as displayed in Table 1, and the results solidly support that the 
3NBCN sample has a preferable application prospect. 
The catalytic properties of GCN and 3NBCN samples were further 
investigated by visible-light-driven photocatalytic removal of Cr(VI). 
The removal curves of Cr(VI) over the GCN and 3NBCN with visible light
