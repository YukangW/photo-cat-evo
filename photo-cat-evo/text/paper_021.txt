A B S T R A C T 

Bulk graphitic carbon nitride suffers from the disadvantages of a small specific surface area, high recombination 
of photogenerated charge carriers, and low surface catalytic activity, which greatly limits its application in 
catalytic reactions. In this study, amino groups were incorporated into the structure of bulk carbon nitride by 
solvothermal treatment facilitated by an optimal content of urea. The experimental data suggested that the 
introduced amino groups modified the morphology and structure of bulk carbon nitride, and improved the 
charge separation. More importantly, the enriched amino groups promoted the formation of nitrogen-rich carbon 
nitride, which was conducive to the deposition and reduction of ultrafine Pt nanoparticles. The resulting Pt/g- 
C3N4 composite exhibited excellent visible-light-driven activity for hydrogen evolution. A high rate of 410.51 
μmol h−1 with an apparent quantum efficiency of 17 % was achieved, which is 85.5 times higher than that of 
bulk carbon nitride. The effective approach developed in this work could provide new insights into the design of 
bulk carbon nitride with enhanced performance. 

1. Introduction 

Over the past decades, the demand for energy and environmental 
concerns have significantly escalated due to the consumption of fossil 
fuels. The development of clean and environmentally friendly energy 
sources is necessary and urgent [1–4]. Semiconductor photocatalysis has 
garnered considerable interest because of its potential to decompose 
water for hydrogen production, convert carbon dioxide into organic 
fuels, degrade organic pollutants, and catalyze organic synthesis [5–9]. 
A carbon-based photocatalyst that has received extensive research 
attention is graphitic carbon nitride (g-C3N4). This is due to its narrow 
bandgap width, appropriate valence band and conducting band positions, two-dimensional layered structure, and robust stability [10–13]. 
However, bulk carbon nitride presents certain drawbacks, including 
high charge-carrier recombination and limited surface active sites, 
which severely restrict its catalytic activity. 

To date, numerous improvement strategies have been proposed to 

resolve the challenges of bulk graphitic carbon nitride (g-C3N4), such as 
nanostructure design, heteroatom doping, semiconductor amalgamation, and noble metal deposition [14–17]. Research shows that introducing surface functional groups is one of the extensively studied 
methods to enhance catalytic performance. Owing to their excellent 
electron-withdrawing abilities, surface functional groups (-OH, –C––O, 
–COOH, –NH2, etc.) are employed to regulate the electronic structure 
and photocatalytic properties of g-C3N4 [18–22]. Among them, surface 
amino group modification of the material for better photocatalytic activity has attracted much attention. Yan et al. reported an 
amino-functionalized ultrathin g-C3N4 with strong redox capability and 
accelerated photocatalytic reaction kinetics. Under the deposition of 3 
wt% Pt cocatalyst, the obtained photocatalyst exhibited an apparent 
quantum efficiency (AQE) of 13.4 % at 420 nm [23]. Huang et al. 
developed a precursor modification strategy to prepare an amino-rich 
thin-pore g-C3N4 with significantly improved performance in the photocatalytic degradation of RhB and hydrogen production (with the

functioned as hole stabilizers, and the introduction of amino groups into 
g-C3N4 is beneficial for prolonging the lifetime of the excited state in the 
photocatalytic reaction [26–29]. 

Although the synthesis and some applications of g-C3N4 with surface 
functional groups have already been reported, the enhancement of 
photocatalytic hydrogen production activity directly by amino groups 
has been limited. In this case, it’s necessary to deposit active centers 
(such as Pt) to achieve efficient photocatalytic hydrogen production 
performance. However, to the best of our knowledge, there has been 
almost no study on the effect of surface amino groups on the morphology 
and structure of the deposited active centers. In this study, amino 
functional groups modified g-C3N4 composites were prepared using a 
urea-induced solvothermal method. The structure and photoelectric 
properties of amino-modified samples were characterized by XRD, 
Raman, FT-IR, XPS, absorption, SEM, TEM, and EIS. The results indicated that the introduction of amino functional groups can not only 
modulate the morphology and structure of g-C3N4, but also improve the 
charge separation. Moreover, the enriched amino groups promoted the 
formation of nitrogen-rich carbon nitride, which facilitated the binding 
or intercalation of exotic Pt atoms into the matrix of g-C3N4 as a high- 
efficiency photocatalytic hydrogen production system. As a result, the 
photocatalytic hydrogen production activity of the obtained Pt/g-C3N4 
experienced a boost under visible light irradiation. 

2. Experiments 

2.1. Preparation of bulk g-C3N4 

All reagents were purchased from Aladdin Biochemical Technology 
Co. and Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co. without further purification. 
Bulk g-C3N4 was prepared using the pyrolysis method [30]. Specifically, 
10 g of melamine was added to an alumina crucible with a lid, heated to 
823 K at an elevated rate of 5 K/min, and kept at this temperature for 2 h 
in air. After cooling to room temperature, the obtained yellow agglomerates were ground into powder for further structural characterization and photocatalytic experiments. The yield of g-C3N4 was 
calculated to be 50 %. 

2.2. Synthesis of amino-modified g-C3N4 

The synthesis of amino-modified g-C3N4 was carried out using the 
solvothermal method. In detail, 50 mg of g-C3N4 was dispersed into 30 
mL of ethylene glycol (EG) and ultrasonicated for 20 min. A certain 
content of urea was then added to the above suspension and ultrasonicated for another 20 min. Subsequently, the suspension was transferred to a 100 mL stainless steel autoclave lined with PTFE and 
maintained at 433 K for 6 h. After cooling naturally to room temperature, the pale yellow precipitate was collected from the autoclave and 
washed several times with ethanol and deionized water. Finally, the 
product was dried at 333 K overnight and ground to a fine powder. The 
yield of amino-modified g-C3N4 was calculated to be 95 %. The obtained 
samples were labeled CNU-x, where x (x = 5, 10, 20, 30, and 50 mg) 
represents the content of urea added. 

2.3. Synthesis of the Pt/g-C3N4 composite 

100 mg of g-C3N4 or CNU-x was dispersed in 30 mL of distilled water, 
and a certain content of H2PtCl6⋅6H2O was added during stirring. The 
mixture was stirred for 5 h in a water bath at 343 K. The samples were 

washed with distilled water and ethanol several times, and then dried 
under vacuum at 333 K to obtain the samples denoted as Pt–CN and 
Pt–CNU-x. Without special instructions, the content of Pt was set at 0.5 
wt%. 

2.4. Characterization 

The Fourier-transform Infrared (FT-IR) spectra were obtained using a 
PerkinElmer Spectrum Two Spectrometer. The crystal structure was 
investigated by powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) in the 5–80◦range using 
a Rigaku Smartlab 9 kW X-ray diffractometer equipped with a Cu Kα 
radiation source. The electronic structure of the samples was recorded 
using an ESCALAB 250Xi X-ray photoelectron spectroscope (XPS). The 
surface structure of the samples was evaluated using a field emission 
scanning electron microscope (SEM, Sirion 200) and a field emission 
transmission electron microscope (JEM-2100F). Raman spectra were 
probed at room temperature using a LabRamHR Evolution spectrometer 
and near-infrared (1064 nm) excitation spectroscopy. The contents of Pt 
were measured using an Optima 7300 DV inductively coupled plasma 
atomic emission spectroscopy (ICP-AES) analyzer. The photocurrent 
responses and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) analyses 
were performed using a CHI-760D electrochemical analyzer. The 
UV–visible diffuse reflectance spectra were recorded using the UV3600- 
MPC3100. The photoluminescent (PL) spectra of the samples were 
analyzed using a fluorescence spectrophotometer (JY Fluorolog-3-Tou). 
Elemental analyses were performed on an elemental analyzer (Vario EL- 
3) for rapid quantitative analysis of elemental content. Contact angle 
test (CA100D, Shanghai Innuo-instruments Co. LTD) was applied to test 
the hydrolysis stability. A fully automated specific surface area and pore 
space analyser (Tristar II 3020 M) was used to record the BET 
measurements. 

2.5. Evaluation of photocatalytic hydrogen production 

Photocatalytic experiments were performed in a gas-sealed recirculation system with external cooling water under visible light irradiation. 
A 300 W xenon lamp (PLS-SXE300D, Beijing PerfectLight Technology 
Co., Ltd.) with a 420 nm cut-off filter was used as the visible light source. 
In a typical photocatalytic hydrogen production experiment, 10 mg of 
photocatalyst powder was dispersed into 100 mL of aqueous solution 
containing 10 vol% triethanolamine (TEOA) as a sacrificial reagent to 
form a homogeneous suspension. Before the photocatalytic reaction, 
high-purity N2 was passed for 20 min to remove the dissolved gas in 
system. 500 μL of the precipitated gas was taken during the photocatalytic reaction, and the content of hydrogen was measured by gas 
chromatography (Kexiao GC1690 gas chromatography equipped with a 
TDX01 column and a thermal conductive detector, and high-purity N2 as 
the carrier gas). Each test was replicated three times, and the mean 
values were calculated. At the end of the reaction, the gas produced in 
the reactor was degassed using high-purity N2. The photocatalytic stability was evaluated using several cycle experiments. 

AQE was measured using the above Xenon lamp with band-pass filters (Beijing PerfectLight). The number of incident photons was detected 
using a FZ-A spectroradiometer (Beijing PerfectLight). The AQE was 
estimated using the following equation (1) [31]:

27.6◦in the XRD spectrum, which correspond to the (100) and (002)

crystal planes. The first peak indicates the characteristic triazine structure with in-plane stacking, whereas the second peak results from the 
interlayer stacking of aromatic structures [32,33]. The diffraction peaks 
indicate that the product was g-C3N4. There were no significant differences in the position of the XRD peaks between g-C3N4 and urea-treated 
g-C3N4. This implies that the characteristic structure of the carbon 
nitride remained intact and was not destroyed by the urea molecules. 
The intensity of the interlayer stacking (002) peak of CNU20 decreased, 
which may be due to the decrease in the content of carbon nitride 
lamellae after the solvent heat treatment of urea. Raman spectra of 
g-C3N4 and urea-treated g-C3N4 were measured using near-infrared 
(1064 nm) excitation to characterize the structures of both materials. 
As shown in Fig. 1(b), the N-symmetric stretching in the s-triazine ring 
structure was the underlying characteristic peak at 709 cm−1, while the 
“melon" structure of g-C3N4 yielded the peak at 1234 cm−1 [34,35]. The 
Raman spectra of urea-treated g-C3N4 remained consistent with those of 
g-C3N4. The findings from the XRD and Raman analyses verified that the 
crystal architecture of g-C3N4 remained unaltered by the urea treatment. 

Fig. 2(a) displays the FT-IR spectra of g-C3N4 and urea-treated g- 
C3N4. The C–N or C––N stretching vibrations were likely the cause of the 
peaks between 1200 and 1650 cm−1 in the g-C3N4. The peak observed at 
810 cm−1 can be ascribed to the tri-s-triazine unit of g-C3N4. The broad 
peaks in the range of 3000–3500 cm−1 are attributed to the stretching of 
N–H bonds [36,37]. From these results, the CNU peaks ranging from 500 
to 2000 cm−1 demonstrate consistency with g-C3N4. Further observation 
of the N–H stretching bands showed (Fig. 2(b)) that the N–H band 
transmittance of CN, CNU10, CNU20, and CNU50 was 78.8, 76.8, 74.4, 
and 72.1 %, respectively. The N–H content of the CNU samples increased 
with higher urea content. This suggests that amino groups were introduced on the surface of carbon nitride by urea treatment. 

The chemical compositions and electronic structures of g-C3N4 and 
CNU20 were analyzed using XPS, as shown in Fig. 3. The high-resolution 
C1s spectra of the samples were presented in Fig. 3(a) and (b), respectively. g-C3N4 was deconvolved into two peaks, whereas CUN20 was 

deconvolved into three peaks. Where standard carbon exhibited a peak 
value of 284.8 eV, corresponding to a graphitic carbon atom, the characteristic peak at 288.3 eV was the result of sp2 hybridized carbon [38, 
39]. In contrast, CNU20 displays a new peak at 286.1 eV, which was 
attributed to the carbon atoms in C–NH, increased to 5.45 % (Table S1) 
[40,41]. The emergence of the new peak implies that amino groups were 
present on the synthesized CNU20 surface. The enhancement of the C–C 
peak at 284.8 eV (from 8.77 % to 11.96 % of the content) corresponds to 
the unavoidable loading of the graphitic carbon atoms, demonstrating 
that the surface structure of g-C3N4 was affected by the urea treatment 
[42]. The N1s spectrum can be separated into four peaks, as shown in 
Fig. 3(c) and (d). The peak at 398.7 eV was related to a two-coordinated 
nitrogen atom in the sp2 hybridized nitrogen, whereas the peak at 399.8 
eV was associated with a three-coordinated nitrogen atom in the sp3 

hybridized nitrogen. The minor peak identified at 401.2 eV was likely 
due to amino functional groups (N–H). The peak at 404.6 eV binding 
energy was attributed to the localization of the positive charge of the 
heptazine ring [43,44]. Compared to g-C3N4, the peaks observed in the 
N1s spectrum of CNU20 were shifted toward a higher binding energy, 
indicating a potential rise in the nitrogen content of the sample. This 
suggests a possible enhanced close interaction between the nitrogen 
atoms and the surrounding carbon atoms. Importantly, the peak at 
401.4 eV (N–H) was enhanced after urea-treated. The N–H contents for 
the g-C3N4 and CNU20 samples were calculated to be 8.27 % and 10.40 
%, respectively (Table S1). The samples underwent contact angle 
testing, and the results are presented in Fig. S1. The gradual reduction in 
the contact angle suggested an increase in the quantity of amino groups. 
This evidence suggested that the introduction of amino groups can increase sample hydrophilicity [27,45]. Meanwhile, we performed 
elemental analysis tests on g-C3N4 and CNU20 (Table S2). The results 
showed that the N/C mass ratio of the treated samples was 1.77, which 
was higher than that of g-C3N4 (1.57) obtained directly from melamine 
without pre-treatment. The results confirmed the successful introduction of the amino groups.

3.2. Photocatalytic H2 production performance 

The performance of g-C3N4 and urea-treated g-C3N4 in photocatalytic hydrogen production was examined, as shown in Fig. 6(a). The 
rate of photocatalytic hydrogen production was remarkably low for g- 
C3N4 at only 4.80 μmol h−1. When amino groups were introduced on its 
surface alone, the hydrogen production rate roughly showed an 
increasing trend, but not a significant increase and the hydrogen production rate of CNU50 was only slightly increased to 6.28 μmol h−1. It 
was shown that the nitrogen-enrich brought about by the amino group is 
positively optimized for the hydrogen production rate of the carbon 
nitride. Although the introduced amino group changes the morphology 
and structure of the bulk carbon nitride and improves the charge separation, the effect on the hydrogen production performance is very 
limited. To the best of our knowledge, there is little research pointing to 
a single amino group that significantly improves the hydrogen production performance of carbon nitride, and most researchers have chosen to 
explore hydrogen production performance with the involvement of a co- 
catalyst. 

Therefore, we also chose to introduce the co-catalyst Pt as the active 
site for hydrogen production, and the hydrogen production performance 
is shown in Fig. 6(b) and (c). When the Pt nanoparticles were deposited, 
the hydrogen production rate was greatly increased compared with that 
of pristine g-C3N4. Due to the deposition of Pt co-catalysts, photogenerated electrons were transferred to the surface of Pt nanoparticles, 
which further promoted the separation of photogenerated electrons and 
holes and effectively inhibited the recombination of photogenerated 
carriers. From the results, it can be seen that the deposition of Pt 
nanoparticles can effectively improve the photocatalytic performance of 
urea-treated carbon nitride. In addition, the hydrogen production rate 
escalates within a specific range as the urea content increases. The 
hydrogen production activity over the Pt–CNU20 composite may be 

influenced by the Pt content, as shown in Fig. 6(d). When the Pt content 
was 0.5 wt% and the urea content was 20 mg, Pt–CNU20 exhibited the 
highest photocatalytic hydrogen production performance, with a rate of 
410.51 μmol h−1, which was 5.5 times greater than that of Pt–CN (74.03 
μmol h−1) and 85.5 times greater than that of g-C3N4 (4.8 μmol h−1). 

The apparent quantum efficiency of the Pt-CUN20 composite was 
measured, as shown in Fig. 7(a). The hydrogen production rate trend 
coincided with the absorption spectrum trend, which decreased as the 
irradiation wavelength increased. The hydrogen production rates were 
211.99, 154.78, 84.12, 40.38, and 12.79 μmol h−1 at 435, 450, 475, 500, 
and 520 nm, respectively. Based on equation (1), the corresponding AQE 
values were 17.0 %, 10.5 %, 3.72 %, 1.68 %, and 0.49 % at 435, 450, 
475, 500, and 520 nm, respectively. The hydrogen production activity of 
Pt–CNU20 was compared with typical Pt/g-C3N4 composite photocatalysts prepared by different methods, and the result in Table S4 
demonstrates significant advantages of our work. When the irradiation 
wavelength reached 550 nm, there was no detectable hydrogen, indicating that the photocatalytic response of the Pt–CNU20 composite in 
visible light resulted from the bandgap transition between the conduction and valence bands. We assessed the endurance of the Pt–CNU20 
composite under visible light exposure for 36 h, as shown in Fig. 7(b). 
The photocatalytic activity remained steady after 6 consecutive rounds 
photocatalytic hydrogen production tests. The structures of the Pt–CNU 
composites after 6 rounds of reaction were characterized by XRD, XPS 
and SEM (Fig. S3). The results showed that the crystal structure, 
morphology and valence state of Pt remained unchanged in the 
Pt–CNU20 composites after the reaction, indicating the stability of the 
Pt–CNU20 composite under the examined conditions.

modification of g-C3N4 with urea can introduce amino groups. However, 
the enhancement of the photocatalytic hydrogen production performance by amino groups directly is very limited. Therefore, we chose to 
introduce the precious metal co-catalyst Pt into this material. The

Fig. S4(a) shows the XRD spectra of Pt–CN and Pt–CNU20 composites. The position of the peaks has not changed compared to the bulk g- 
C3N4. The intensity of the (002) peak for interlayer stacking in 
Pt–CNU20 composite was lower than that of Pt–CN, which was in line 
with the findings for CNU20. This reduction was due to the decrease in 
the g-C3N4 lamellar structure content caused by the heat treatment of 
the urea solvent. Furthermore, the characteristic diffraction peak of Pt 
was not found in Fig. S4(a). This may be due to the low content of Pt, 
making it difficult to observed. Alternatively, it could be due to the 
overlap of the main diffraction peak of Pt with that of g-C3N4. The 
structures of Pt–CN and Pt–CNU20 composites were characterized by 
measuring their Raman spectra under near-infrared (1064 nm) excitation, as shown in Fig. S4(b). The Raman spectra of Pt after deposition are 
generally consistent with those of g-C3N4, indicating that low content of 
Pt was indeed difficult to detect by XRD and Raman. 

The XPS spectra of Pt 4f in the Pt–CN and Pt–CNU20 composites are 
shown in Fig. 8 and Table S5. For the Pt–CN composite, six peaks can be 
fitted to the Pt 4f spectrum. The peaks with binding energies of 72.2 and 
74.9 eV were attributed to metallic Pt0 (19.38 %), those with binding 
energies of 72.8 and 76.0 eV were attributed to the oxidation state of 
Pt2+ (57.39 %), and those with binding energies of 74.2 and 78.2 eV 
were attributed to the oxidation state of Pt4+ (23.23 %) [51,52]. Significant changes in the properties of Pt due to surface charge redistribution occurred when g-C3N4 was modified with urea. A substantial 
increase in the percentage of metallic Pt0 (37.26 %) was observed. At the 

same time, the peaks corresponding to the different valence states of Pt 
were red-shifted, moving to a higher binding energy. This was consistent 
with the red-shifted of the peaks in the N1s spectrum, as shown in Fig. 8 
(b). It is demonstrated that the presence of amino groups enhances Pt–N 
coordination. The enhanced Pt–N coordination bond facilitates photogenerated charge migration from g-C3N4 to Pt. 

According to the ICP-AES measurements (Table S5), the Pt–CN 
composite had an actual Pt content of 0.15 wt%, which was significantly 
lower than the target content of 0.5 wt%. The loading of Pt in the 
Pt–CNU20 composite material was 0.33 wt%, which was twice as high as 
that in the Pt–CN composite. Thus, the introduction of amino groups can 
improve the reduction efficiency, which can reduce most of the Pt4+ in 
H2PtCl6 to Pt0. The metallic Pt0 deposited onto the g-C3N4 surface enhances the photocatalytic hydrogen production performance. This is 
because metallic Pt0 can provide more photocatalytic active sites, which 
enhances the efficiency of the reduction reaction of water molecules to 
produce hydrogen. The presence of Ptδ+ does not diminish the active site 
for producing photocatalytic hydrogen, and it can act as the active 
center for holes, aiding in the separation of photogenerated carriers. 
Meanwhile, oxidized Pt2+ performs a critical function in inhibiting unwanted hydrogen back-oxidation [53–55]. Therefore, it can be demonstrated that the Pt–CNU20 photocatalyst with a larger proportion of 
metallic Pt0 is more effective in hydrogen production than Pt–CN. 

Fig. 9 shows the TEM images of Pt–CN and Pt–CNU20 composites. 
The image shows that Pt nanoparticles with an average size of 1–2 nm 
are uniformly distributed on the surface of CN. In the STEM image of the

3.4. Mechanism of photocatalysis 

To identify the factors behind the significant improvement in the 
photocatalytic hydrogen production performance of Pt–CNU composites, the possible mechanisms are presented in Fig. 10. The presence of 
amino groups facilitates the improvement of the charge separation of g- 
C3N4 and contributes to the prolongation of the lifetime of the excited 
state in the photocatalytic reaction. The amino-rich functional groups 
facilitate the binding of Pt atoms, and enhance Pt–N coordination. 
Moreover, the introduction of amino groups enables the reduction of 
Pt4+ and further hinders the growth of Pt nanoparticles, because of the 
improved Pt–N coordination. Visible light irradiation prompts g-C3N4 to 
generate photogenerated electrons (e−) and holes (h+) at CB and VB, 
correspondingly. The electrons produced at CB are quickly conveyed to 
the surface of the metallic Pt0 to achieve effective electron-hole separation. Subsequently, the H+ in the water molecule can receive electrons 
from the metallic Pt0 to create hydrogen. Meanwhile, the TEOA sacrificial agent utilizes the holes moved to Ptδ+ in the VB through an 
oxidation reaction. 

4. Conclusions 

In summary, the structure of bulk carbon nitride was altered by the 
amino groups introduced through urea treatment. The increase in hydrophilicity facilitated the adsorption of H+. The enriched amino groups 
also contributed to the deposition and reduction of ultrafine Pt nanoparticles, effectively preventing their agglomeration or deactivation. 
Furthermore, the presence of amino groups improved the reduction of 
Pt4+ and increased the amount of deposited Pt. The findings demonstrate that Pt–CNU20 composites exhibit exceptional photocatalytic 
activity for hydrogen production, surpassing the performance of previously reported g–C3N4–based catalysts. The enhanced visible light 
trapping ability, effective separation of photogenerated charge carriers, 
and deposition of ultrafine Pt nanoparticles are the reasons for the superior photocatalytic performance. The effective approach developed in 
this work could provide new insights into the design of bulk carbon 
nitride with enhanced performance.
